I open my “Poetry for Programmers” course by pulling up the Visual Studio Code interface—dark mode, with neon highlights. In windows next to each other are two files: CP40-58m14b.xml and CP40-58m14b.jpg. The file on the left is a vibrant stack of coordinates interlaced with white text—all written in a language many students will eventually recognize as Latin. On the right is a just-shy-of-pixelated photograph of a tattered medieval manuscript, covered in arcane lines of script. I ask them to use the XML transcript to help them decipher the paleographical text. By the end of a few minutes, most have engaged in paleography for the first time. I tell them that digital tools and antique expertise have much to teach each other—the patterns of poetic structure can shape our minds as effectively as the patterns of digital interactions shape our online lives. Each skill is technical, and by putting them in concert, we can make profound new insights into both computer vision and medieval paleography.

My teaching philosophy rests on the supposition that while tools change, the human desire for narrative pattern and exception remains stable. Whether teaching students to analyze a sonnet or apply machine learning methods to civic problems, I aim to show that with a robust imagination, we can rediscover innovative methods by carefully examining the history of technology. For instance, I have developed a course on “medieval AI,” which examines the history of literary engagements with infinite-knowledge technology. It is my responsibility to teach students not just to be users, but to be delightedly human in environments often hostile to that delight. My teaching fosters a rapid sense of belonging that affirms students—whether they are approaching DNID from the perspective of English, Information Science, or Studio Arts—because of their diverse backgrounds, not in spite of them.

I have a hard-earned sense for the instructional strategies that successfully engage the mix of computer scientists, engineers, artists, and humanists that the Digital Narrative and Interactive Design (DNID) program attracts. I develop courses that explicitly address the clash of disciplinary intuitions, teaching students how to articulate expertise for their collaborators—a skill essential for effective teamwork between literary scholars and developers. My teaching is shaped by the belief that computational techniques give humanists effective tools to solve problems, while humanities training gives developers the critical framework to understand why they are building.

To bridge these gaps, I emphasize the materiality of our tools. My own practice of repairing vintage film cameras and building mechanical keyboards informs my teaching; I use these physical examples to demonstrate that hardware, like software, is a designed narrative with specific constraints and possibilities. Readings like John Vincler’s “Grid Logic,” written on Susan Howe’s experimental poetry, demonstrate the essential role of the “hardware” like pens, typewriters, computers on the creative process. While human creation is not fully dependent on these tools, there is a strong correlative relationship between how one designs a text and the tools creators use to make them Design and UX/UI students in my course often remark that our discussion of Don Norman’s The Design of Everyday Things prepares them to engage more deeply with their major courses. By applying Norman's concepts of affordances and signifiers to the "interface" of a 14th-century manuscript or the tactile feedback of a pen, I help students develop a designer's eye for how information is surfaced or hidden in any medium.

I take a design-centric approach to pedagogy; each class asks for “user feedback” on the materials, order of concepts, and design of course websites and assignments. Students learn that courses are themselves carefully designed, and I at once offer them the tools to understand and critique interactive design while asking them to apply it to the course that teaches them these skills. I have developed and offered ten iterations of a mini called ‘Poetry for Programmers,’ which integrates formal poetics with the philosophy of computer science. The course centers around interdisciplinary collaboration. We look at a poem by Pittsburgh poet Cameron Barnett where the stanzas all begin with a 1 or 0 in an apparently random order. In every section I have taught, a CS student suddenly realizes that the sequence is the ASCII binary for “I”—the very coded subject of poem. The poem is a sort of code that produces the speaker’s self as they read. After contextualizing this with historical readings about the Jacquard loom and woven RAM, I show students that computation is not just a method of analysis but a deeply human activity. 

This focus on structure translates directly to interactive spaces. Beyond analysis, I guide students through the complex logic of narrative branching and digital prototyping. I encourage students to engage creatively with traditional research projects; for instance, I mentored a student who learned Unity to write a video game that explored narrative choice mechanics in medieval literature. We worked together to map the conditional logic of the game's dialogue trees, translating the allegories of the source text into rigid Boolean constraints. This process helped the student understand that "interactive narrative" is not just about writing a story, but about architecting the system in which that story lives. Creating an adaptation is also not a neutral act; like translation or summary, adapting a story requires choice and selection, which are only consistent and moving if they follow loving interpretation.

My work on Glyph Machina has directly involved me in the kind of high-level research this program seeks to include students in and can serve as a model project. Glyph Machina has a field-defining Handwritten Character Recognition (HCR) model that beats all current known models, utilizing a Convolutional Neural Network (CNN) connected to LSTMs, implemented in PyTorch. This project gives me experience in humanities-oriented applications of Artificial Intelligence and Machine Learning as well as Data Modeling and Computational Linguistics—expertise that helps me guide students in the production of highly technical projects. My research on the impact of GM's output for Large Language Models (LLMs) builds curricula on ethical AI development and historical data bias, preparing students to value the long history of human originality in a computational age.
