## Teaching Philosophy

I open my "Poetry for Programmers" course by pulling up the Visual Studio Code interface—dark mode, with neon highlights. In windows next to each other are two files: CP40-58m14b.xml and CP40-58m14b.jpg. The file on the left is a vibrant stack of coordinates interlaced with white text—all written in a language many students will eventually recognize as Latin. On the right is a just-shy-of-pixelated photograph of a tattered medieval manuscript, covered in arcane lines of script. I ask them to use the XML transcript to help them decipher the paleographical text. By the end of a few minutes, most have engaged in paleography for the first time. I tell them that digital tools and antique expertise have much to teach each other—the patterns of poetic structure can shape our minds as effectively as the patterns of digital interactions shape our online lives. Each skill is technical, and by putting them in concert, we can make profound new insights into both computer vision and medieval paleography.

My teaching philosophy rests on the supposition that humans remain relatively stable in the face of technological shifts—and that it is my responsibility to teach them not how to be a new kind of human but to be delightedly human in environments often hostile to that delight. My teaching is shaped by the belief that computational techniques give humanists effective tools to solve problems they can well articulate. Whether teaching students to analyze a sonnet or apply machine learning methods to civic problems, I aim to show that with a robust imagination, we can rediscover innovative methods by carefully examining the history of technology. For instance, I have developed a course on "medieval AI," which examines the history of literary engagements with infinite-knowledge technology. My teaching fosters a rapid sense of belonging that affirms students—from English, computer science, the arts, and other disciplines—because of their diverse backgrounds, not in spite of them.

To bridge disciplinary gaps, I emphasize the materiality of our tools. My own practice of repairing vintage film cameras and building mechanical keyboards informs my teaching; I use these physical examples to demonstrate that hardware, like software, is a designed narrative with specific constraints and possibilities. Readings like John Vincler's "Grid Logic," written on Susan Howe's experimental poetry, demonstrate the essential role of the "hardware" like pens, typewriters, computers on the creative process. While human creation is not fully dependent on these tools, there is a strong correlative relationship between how one designs a text and the tools creators use to make them. I teach that core coding concepts—the DRY Principle (Don't Repeat Yourself) and orthogonality—apply to writing efficient papers and structuring effective paragraphs. Design and UX/UI students in my course often remark that our discussion of Don Norman's *The Design of Everyday Things* prepares them to engage more deeply with their major courses. By applying Norman's concepts of affordances and signifiers to the "interface" of a 14th-century manuscript or the tactile feedback of a pen, I help students develop a designer's eye for how information is surfaced or hidden in any medium.

I take a design-centric approach to pedagogy; each class asks for "user feedback" on the materials, order of concepts, and design of course websites and assignments. Students learn that courses are themselves carefully designed, and I at once offer them the tools to understand and critique interactive design while asking them to apply it to the course that teaches them these skills. I have developed and offered ten iterations of a mini called 'Poetry for Programmers,' which integrates formal poetics with the philosophy of computer science. The course centers around interdisciplinary collaboration. We look at a poem by Pittsburgh poet Cameron Barnett where the stanzas all begin with a 1 or 0 in an apparently random order. In every section I have taught, a CS student suddenly realizes that the sequence is the ASCII binary for "I"—the very coded subject of poem. The poem is a sort of code that produces the speaker's self as they read. After contextualizing this with historical readings about the Jacquard loom and woven RAM on the Apollo module, I show students that computation is not just a method of analysis but, like meter, is a very human activity. Complex decision-making, students learn, is similarly the result of a long series of small binary decisions.

This focus on structure translates directly to interactive spaces. Beyond analysis, I guide students through the complex logic of narrative branching and digital prototyping. I encourage students to engage creatively with traditional research projects; for instance, I mentored a student who learned Unity to write a video game that explored narrative choice mechanics in medieval literature. We worked together to map the conditional logic of the game's dialogue trees, translating the allegories of the source text into rigid Boolean constraints. This process helped the student understand that "interactive narrative" is not just about writing a story, but about architecting the system in which that story lives. Creating an adaptation is also not a neutral act; like translation or summary, adapting a story requires choice and selection, which are only consistent and moving if they follow loving interpretation.

My work on Glyph Machina has involved me in high-level research that can serve as a model project for students. Glyph Machina has a field-defining Handwritten Character Recognition (HCR) model that beats all current known models, utilizing a Convolutional Neural Network (CNN) connected to LSTMs, implemented in PyTorch. This project gives me experience in humanities-oriented applications of Artificial Intelligence and Machine Learning as well as Data Modeling and Computational Linguistics—expertise that helps me guide students in the production of highly technical projects. My research on the impact of GM's output for Large Language Models (LLMs) builds curricula on ethical AI development and historical data bias. Combining technical skills with an interpretive mindset is essential to distinguish propaganda from promise and hallucinations from meaningful analysis in an increasingly AI-driven world.

The success of this approach is evidenced by student outcomes and feedback. My courses have been named in campus tours as positively engaging students' chosen majors. A mechanical engineering student wrote: "I gained more from this course than I ever would have expected from a mandatory first year college course. The improvement I observed in my ability to effectively analyze and digest texts, especially poetry, was remarkable for such a short period of time." A statistics/machine learning major commented: "I spent 7 years in a creative writing program and I think I appreciate poetry more now than I ever did during that." Two students from "Poetry for Programmers" have had papers selected for publication by WOVEN, the undergraduate writing journal. I have mentored students who learned Unity to write video games engaging medieval literature, developed ML models to predict food shortages, and critically implemented AI tools in research projects.

I take design and poetics as the twin pillars of my instruction philosophy and course structure. My experience as a team member on digital humanities projects over the last decade has shown me the value of using technical communications and business strategies to help humanists articulate communications needs to computer scientists and vice-versa. This experience has shaped my understanding of design language as a tool for an interdisciplinary collaboration that retains pedagogically productive senses of 'play' in educational settings. Poetry, by contrast, offers granular engagement and emphasizes the value of structure. Furthermore, since one of poetry's primary characteristics is compression—i.e. that it is, in a sense, simply communication and context compressed—it serves as a highly effective tool for teaching composition and metacognition. In contemporary composition classrooms, especially in models that focus on practice and rhetorical theory, poetry can serve as an effective spot-lesson for student engagement without being itself the subject of the course. By thinking about documents as designed objects and language as variously capable of compression, I help students develop a combination of practical skills (e.g. what is a paragraph designed to do?) and metacognitive awareness (e.g. what tools do I use to determine a writer's motivation?) essential to interpreting a wide range of documents. I developed this philosophy for Carnegie Mellon to account for the fact that its students range from computer scientists, biologists, and mechanical engineers to artists, actors, and musicians. Each of these students has a dedicated technical investment, and I offer them a pathway into writing and research through the lens of their chosen disciplines.

I am invested in student buy-in and encourage interdisciplinary work within the course and across the university. My course has been specifically named in campus tours as positively engaging students' chosen majors in a class with a unified theme. In 76-106, "Poetry for Programmers," I designed a course to engage CMU's diverse student population and to foster metacognition and interdisciplinary thinking. As an initial engagement in close reading, I have students prepare close reading notes that encourage Pattern + Interpretation interpretive skills. I give them two poems in the first week—the first is Cameron Barnett's "Ones and Zeros" which is a poem that outlines a speaker's struggle with self-expression. Barnett writes:

> 0.  
> 	I wanted to write something  
> 	quaint and coded, but all  
> 	I could manage  
> 	was the letter  
> 	"I" on one side  
> 	of a page.

Students ponder what it means to write something 'coded,'—a hint at a heuristic and a frustration with clear expression. The stanzas all begin with a 1 or 0 in an apparently random order. In small groups, students look for repeating features and differences in those patterns—a skill that they will continue to develop to produce their CRA. I chose this poem to encourage interdisciplinary cooperation; since the words 'programmer' and 'poetry' both appear in the course title, students often select this mini because they have investments in literature or in computer science.

In these small groups, they begin, on the second day of class, to genuinely appreciate the skills and disciplinary knowledge of their peers. When they turn to discussion as a whole, students leverage their pre-existing disciplinary knowledge to aid each others' interpretations. Students who are comfortable with metaphor help explain what it might be for the "body" to be a "sentence" and to think about how Barnett is helping them reimagine something like a 'body paragraph' as metaphorically helpful for understanding the different parts of an essay as not arbitrary but fundamentally human. Usually, however, the profound 'ah-ha' moment comes from a computer scientist. Metaphorically-minded students often try to interpret the pattern of ones and zeros, which is 01001001, as thematic stanza by stanza, but the literary patterns they are used to slowly fail them. In each section I have taught, one CS student will sit upright and suddenly frantically search something on his or her phone or laptop. What they learn is that 01001001 is binary for "I"—the only thing that the speaker says he could manage to say. The thing that is quaint and coded, merely the letter I, is the whole poem, itself acting like a sort of code to produce an image of the speaker's self.

From the first week of the class, students learn that collaboration and disciplinary knowledge are tools for profound insight. Knowledge of binary has interpretive and structural importance in our contemporary expressive landscape, and all students together learn that "study of poetic metaphors also teaches student writers about the power of restatement to clarify, enhance, and explain." I encourage students in the class to continue to leverage their disciplinary strengths to build up confidence and knowledge banks in their peers. As one student said at the end of a semester: "Ones and Zeros" was "An excellent introduction to the class; I personally love poems that are related to science or existentialism and love in some way, so the very first poem we looked at has still stuck with me" and linked it to the logic unit later in the course, saying "I enjoy applying logic to written sentences"—but initial results point to significantly improved skill acquisition.
