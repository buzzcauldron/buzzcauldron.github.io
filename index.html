About

I open my "Poetry for Programmers" course by pulling up the Visual Studio Code interface—dark mode, with neon highlights. In windows next to each other are two files: CP40-58m14b.xml and CP40-58m14b.jpg. The file on the left is a vibrant stack of coordinates interlaced with white text—all written in a language many students will eventually recognize as Latin. On the right is a just-shy-of-pixelated photograph of a tattered medieval manuscript, covered in arcane lines of script. I ask them to use the XML transcript to help them decipher the paleographical text. By the end of a few minutes, most have engaged in paleography for the first time. I tell them that digital tools and antique expertise have much to teach each other—the patterns of poetic structure can shape our minds as effectively as the patterns of digital interactions shape our online lives.

My teaching philosophy rests on the supposition that while tools change, the human desire for narrative pattern and exception remains stable. Whether teaching students to analyze a sonnet or apply machine learning methods to civic problems, I aim to show that with a robust imagination, we can rediscover innovative methods by carefully examining the history of technology. For instance, I have developed a course on "medieval AI," which examines the history of literary engagements with infinite-knowledge technology. My teaching fosters a rapid sense of belonging that affirms students—whether they are approaching DNID from the perspective of English, Information Science, or Studio Arts—because of their diverse backgrounds, not in spite of them.

I take a design-centric approach to pedagogy; each class asks for "user feedback" on the materials, order of concepts, and design of course websites and assignments. I have developed and offered ten iterations of a mini called 'Poetry for Programmers,' which integrates formal poetics with the philosophy of computer science. We look at a poem by Pittsburgh poet Cameron Barnett where the stanzas all begin with 1 or 0—the ASCII binary for "I," the very coded subject of the poem. My work on Glyph Machina involves a field-defining Handwritten Character Recognition (HCR) model, utilizing CNNs connected to LSTMs in PyTorch—experience that helps me guide students in humanities-oriented AI/ML and ethical development.
