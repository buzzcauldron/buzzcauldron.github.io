Helpful Poem

Ones and Zeros by Pittsburgh poet Cameron Barnett—used in 76-106 Poetry for Programmers. Each stanza begins with a 1 or 0; the sequence spells the letter “I” in binary.

0.
I wanted to write something
quaint and coded, but all
I could manage
was the letter
“I” on one side
of a page.

1.
My body is a sentence
waiting for its period.

0.
My mouth, a comma
stuck between breath
and what comes next.

0.
I am the ellipsis
at the end of my own story.

1.
I am the question mark
that never finds its answer.

0.
I am the semicolon
connecting what I cannot say.

0.
I am the colon
introducing what I refuse to name.

1.
I wanted to write something
quaint and coded, but all
I could manage
was the letter
“I” on one side
of a page.

About

. The file on the left is a vibrant stack of coordinates interlaced with white text—all written in a language many students will eventually recognize as Latin. On the right is a just-shy-of-pixelated photograph of a tattered medieval manuscript, covered in arcane lines of script. I ask them to use the XML transcript to help them decipher the paleographical text. By the end of a few minutes, most have engaged in paleography for the first time. I tell them that digital tools and antique expertise have much to teach each other—the patterns of poetic structure can shape our minds as effectively as the patterns of digital interactions shape our online lives.

My teaching philosophy rests on the supposition that humans remain relatively stable in the face of technological shifts—and that it is my responsibility to teach them not how to be a new kind of human but to be delightedly human in environments often hostile to that delight. My teaching is shaped by the belief that computational techniques give humanists effective tools to solve problems they can well articulate. I teach that core coding concepts—the DRY Principle (Don't Repeat Yourself) and orthogonality—apply to writing efficient papers and structuring effective paragraphs; design and UX/UI students often remark that our discussion of Don Norman's The Design of Everyday Things prepares them to engage more deeply with their major courses. My teaching fosters a rapid sense of belonging that affirms students—from English, computer science, the arts, and other disciplines—because of their diverse backgrounds, not in spite of them.

I take a design-centric approach to pedagogy; each class asks for "user feedback" on the materials, order of concepts, and design of course websites and assignments. I have developed and offered ten iterations of a mini called 'Poetry for Programmers,' which integrates formal poetics with the philosophy of computer science. We look at a poem by Pittsburgh poet Cameron Barnett where the stanzas all begin with 1 or 0—the ASCII binary for "I," the very coded subject of the poem. After contextualizing with historical readings about the Jacquard loom and woven RAM on the Apollo module, I show students that computation is not just a method of analysis but, like meter, is a very human activity. Combining technical skills with an interpretive mindset is essential to distinguish propaganda from promise and hallucinations from meaningful analysis in an increasingly AI-driven world. My work on Glyph Machina involves a field-defining Handwritten Character Recognition (HCR) model, utilizing CNNs connected to LSTMs in PyTorch—experience that helps me guide students in humanities-oriented AI/ML and ethical development.
